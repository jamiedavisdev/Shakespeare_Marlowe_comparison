A computational test · 56 plays · five methods · three corpora

Marlowe or Shakespeare?

Christopher Marlowe died in a Deptford tavern in May 1593. Within a year or two a playwright named William Shakespeare began to be printed. A long-running theory says they were the same man. We asked what the texts themselves say when you measure them.

Our first plan was to train a language model on each author and compare their weights. Two models trained on the same author came out no more alike (0.27) than two trained on different authors (0.27), so that method cannot answer the question.

Short answer: the measurements do not support one author. Marlowe's style does live on in one place, the Henry VI plays, but there it is shared by half a dozen other writers of 1587 to 1592. Scroll for the ideas, the methods, and every result, with controls you can play with.

I · The question

Two canons that almost touch

Marlowe wrote seven surviving plays between about 1587 and 1593. Shakespeare's earliest plays date from 1591 or so. The theory needs the two bodies of work to be one continuous voice. So the fair test is not "does Marlowe sound like Hamlet" but "does Marlowe sound like Shakespeare's work up to 1594, and does he sound more like it than other writers of exactly the same years do?"

That last clause is the whole game. Between 1587 and 1592 a dozen playwrights wrote blank-verse heroic drama in the manner Marlowe made famous. If a test cannot tell Marlowe from Robert Greene or George Peele, it cannot tell us anything about Shakespeare either.

II · The original idea

Train two language models and compare their weights

The first proposal was elegant: fine-tune one language model on Marlowe, one on early Shakespeare, and see whether the weights end up in the same place. If one person wrote both, the two models should have learned the same thing.

We tried it, and we built the controls that tell you whether it could work. The key question is not "how similar are the Marlowe and Shakespeare models?" but "how similar are two models trained on the same author, and is that any closer than two trained on different authors?" If same-author pairs are no closer than different-author pairs, the method has no author signal, whatever number it gives for the pair you care about.

1 · One starting pointGPT-2 small, pretrained. Every model begins from the same weights.
2 · Equal dietsThirteen models, each fed exactly 78,818 tokens, an equal share from every play in its set, three passes.
3 · Measure the changeSubtract the original weights from each. That difference is what the model learned.
4 · Compare with controlsCosine similarity between the differences, including same-text/new-seed and same-author/other-plays pairs.

How similar are the weight changes?

Cosine similarity between fine-tuning deltas. Hover a cell. Rows and columns are the thirteen models.

Read it like this. The same text trained with a different random seed gives 0.75 to 0.78. Everything else, same author or not, same register or not, sits at 0.19 to 0.30. Two Marlowe models trained on different halves of his canon (0.27) are no closer than Marlowe and Greene (0.27). The weights record which text the model saw. They do not record who wrote it. The Marlowe versus early Shakespeare pair (0.23) lands with the different-register pairs.

So the original idea gives a clear answer, just not to the question it was meant for. It tells us that at this scale weight comparison carries no author signal. We then turned to methods that have a track record, and we kept the same discipline: every result has to be read against a same-author benchmark and a different-author benchmark.

III · How we tested

Measure the habits, not the poetry

Authorship tests work on the words a writer does not think about: the, and, of, to, which, that. Everyone uses them, nobody chooses them deliberately, and their rates are remarkably stable for one person across subjects and genres. The big words, the imagery and the characters belong to the play. The little words belong to the writer.

Function words

About 190 grammatical words. Each play becomes a profile of how often it uses each one, standardised across the corpus.

Three rules we held to

Controls first. Every number is shown beside what the same test gives for two sets by one author (Shakespeare early vs late, Marlowe early vs late) and for two sets by different authors in the same style (Marlowe vs Greene, Greene vs Peele).

Families stay together. Henry VI 1 to 3, Richard III and the anonymous True Tragedy of Richard III share characters and plot, so they are always held out together. Likewise the two Tamburlaines, the two Shrews, and The Spanish Tragedy with Soliman and Perseda.

Three corpora. Modern editions can carry an editor's spelling habits. We rebuilt the whole study on texts that all came through one pipeline (the 1623 Folio and 1590s quartos from EarlyPrint), and again on Shakespeare's early quartos only. Results that did not replicate were thrown out.

V · Verification

Could this play be by Marlowe?

The impostors method asks a yes-or-no question per play. In each of 200 rounds it draws a random half of the features and twelve random "impostor" plays, then checks whether the target is nearer to some play by the candidate than to every impostor. The score is the share of rounds that say yes. A play by a known author should score high in its own column and low elsewhere, and the known authors tell us what "high" means here.

Two things stand out. Marlowe's own plays verify as Marlowe at only about 0.5, and as Peele at about the same. That is the ceiling of this method inside the heroic register: it simply cannot tell those writers apart. Against that ceiling, Shakespeare's non-Henry VI early plays score 0.05 as Marlowe and 0.89 as Shakespeare, lower as Marlowe than Greene's plays do (0.35). The Henry VI plays score 0.55 as Marlowe, 0.53 as anonymous heroic, 0.38 as Shakespeare. They sit in the crowd.

VI · Classifiers

Can a machine tell them apart?

A classifier is trained on 2,000-word chunks from two sets of plays, then tested on a play it has never seen. If two sets are by one writer the accuracy should hover near coin-flip, 0.5. If they are by different writers it should climb toward 1. The benchmark pairs tell us where "near 0.5" and "near 1" fall for plays of this period and size.

Marlowe vs early Shakespeare without Henry VI scores 0.85, above every same-author pair and above Marlowe vs Greene (0.59) or Marlowe vs Peele (0.60). Marlowe vs the Henry VI plays and Titus scores 0.43, below chance, which is what happens when two sets share a style and the classifier latches on to plays rather than authors. Note that Greene vs Peele also scores only 0.60: this test, like the others, is weak inside the heroic register.

VII · Character models

Letter by letter, and a lesson about editions

A character model learns how six-letter sequences follow one another in an author's text. Ask it to predict a new play and it needs a certain number of bits per character; the fewer, the more the play "sounds like" the training author. We trained one model per author on equal amounts of text, with all proper names masked so that Tamburlaine does not give itself away.

This was where our first report went wrong. On the mixed-edition corpus the Marlowe model recognised Marlowe's plays, and we said so. Then we measured what happens when the same play is swapped for another edition: the cost moved by 0.04 to 0.07 bits per character, the same size as the author effect. On the single-source corpus the recognition vanished.

The Marlowe gap, play by play

Cost under the Marlowe model minus cost under the later-Shakespeare model, bits per character. Left of zero: the Marlowe model predicts the play better. Hover for values.

Greene's and Peele's plays are predicted better by the Marlowe model than Marlowe's own plays are. That is the signature of a register detector, not an author detector. Within that limit the early Shakespeare plays sit between the heroic register and later Shakespeare, Titus nearest the register and Richard III nearest later Shakespeare, which is what chronology and genre would predict for a different author, and is also compatible with one author changing. The character models do not decide the question. On quartos only, Titus and Richard III move toward Marlowe by a few hundredths, and still verify as Shakespeare at 0.91 and as Marlowe at 0.07.

VIII · Scene by scene

Inside Henry VI

Scholars have long suspected the Henry VI plays are collaborations. The 2016 New Oxford Shakespeare credits Marlowe as co-author of all three. Craig and Kinney found Marlowe's hand specifically in the Jack Cade rebellion scenes of Part 2. We scored every scene with two scorers trained on four authors, with the scanned play's whole family held out of training.

Scene-level probability of Marlowe

Function-word classifier, four candidates: Marlowe, Shakespeare, Peele, Greene. Bar height is P(Marlowe); hover for the full split. Shaded scenes carry a published attribution.

The Cade scenes (4.2 to 4.10 of Part 2) lean Marlowe-ward: P(Marlowe) 0.56 against 0.23 for the rest of the play, and the lean survives when verse is compared only with verse (0.71 against 0.33). That matches the published finding. Nothing comparable appears in Richard III (0.10) or Titus (0.08). A first-pass pattern in Part 1 that matched the Nashe/Shakespeare division did not survive the verse-only check, so we do not claim it.

X · What went wrong, and what we changed

The notebook, not just the report

A study like this is only as good as its willingness to break its own results. These are the things that broke.

Version 1 overstated the character-model evidence

We claimed that "Marlowe's plays recognise each other". The claim rested on comparing texts from different editorial pipelines. Measuring the edition effect directly showed it to be as large as the author effect. The claim is withdrawn; word-level tests were unaffected and replicate across corpora.

A training leak in the first scene scan

Titus was scored by a model that had seen Titus, producing an absurd result. Every scan now trains fresh models with the scanned play's whole family removed.

Budget truncation biased the early-Shakespeare model

The first character-model run took plays in list order until the budget ran out, so "early Shakespeare" was effectively Henry VI plus Titus. Every model now draws an equal share from every play.

Proper-noun masking ate the pronoun "I"

Capitalisation-based masking treated I and I'll as names. Fixed by exclusion.

Edward II lost its opening speech

The EarlyPrint parser only collected marked speeches; some early quartos have bare lines before the first speaker label. The parser now collects them.

What would add real information

A method that can split the heroic register. Word-adjacency networks or Burrows's Zeta, accepted only if they first separate Greene from Peele, two writers we know are different. Until something passes that test, nobody's method can say who wrote the Cade scenes.

More tokens per author for the neural models, run on quarto texts only, to see whether the Henry VI result holds up.

Limits to keep in mind

Seven Marlowe plays, two of them damaged texts. Eight early Shakespeare plays, three probably collaborative. Four plays each for Greene and Peele. Every single test here is weak at this size. The strength of the conclusion comes from the same pattern appearing in every method, on every corpus.

If one person had written both canons, Marlowe's hand should be visible across early Shakespeare, more visible there than in Greene and Peele, and visible in the comedies and in Richard III, not only in the plays scholars already consider collaborative. None of that holds.REPORT.md, version 2

XI · Glossary

Terms used on this page

Function words

Grammatical words with little meaning of their own: articles, prepositions, pronouns, conjunctions, auxiliaries. Their frequencies are the backbone of modern authorship attribution.

Cosine delta

A distance between two plays' standardised word-frequency profiles. Zero means identical profiles; values around 1 are typical between unrelated plays here.

Leave-one-play-out

Train on every play except one, test on that one, repeat. It stops a classifier from memorising the play it is being tested on.

AUC

Area under the ROC curve: the chance that a random chunk from set A is scored more A-like than a random chunk from set B. 0.5 is coin-flip, 1 is perfect.

General Impostors

A verification method (Koppel and Winter, 2014). It measures how often a text is nearer to a candidate author than to random impostors, across many random subsets of features.

Bits per character / per token

How surprised a model is by each new character or word-piece. Lower means the text is more like what the model trained on.

Register

A shared style of writing, here the blank-verse heroic drama of 1587 to 1592 that Marlowe popularised and others imitated.

Task vector / weight delta

The difference between a fine-tuned model's weights and the pretrained model's. Cosine similarity between two such vectors measures whether two fine-tunings pushed the model in the same direction.

Folio and quarto

Quartos are the small, cheap individual play editions of the 1590s. The Folio is the large 1623 collected edition of Shakespeare, printed seven years after his death.
